# Packet Switched Networks
Carrier packet switched networks were the pre-cursor of the modern Internet. Users share common carrier resources rather than having dedicated resources. Because this allows the carrier to make more efficient use of its infrastructure, the cost to the customer was generally much better than with point-to-point lines. In a packet switching setup, networks have connections into the carrier's network, and many customers share the carrier's network. The carrier can then create virtual circuits between customer’s sites by which packets of data are delivered from one to the other through the network.

## X.25 
In the early days of packet switched WANs, X. 25 networks were provided by carriers to allow for basic packet switched connections. Networks at the time were slow and unreliable and the protocol was built robustly to work in these conditions. In some niche industries like Aerospace, you may still see X.25.

## Frame Relay
Frame Relay succeeded X.25 and was designed for speed over more reliable networks. In application, we used it as a one technology to interconnect LANs. It was a packet switched technology, and it was developed because of higher data rates and lower error rates in 1990s high speed (!) communications systems. Information was sent in variable length packets (frames) through a shared frame relay network. Each frame contained all the information necessary to route it to the correct destination. Each Data Termination Equipment (DTE) endpoint could communicate with many destinations over one access link to the network.

Some new concepts were introduced; Switched Virtual Circuits (SVCs) were temporary connections used in situations requiring only sporadic data transfer between DTE devices across the Frame Relay network. Permanent Virtual Circuits (PVCs) established connections that were used for frequent and consistent data transfers between DTE devices across the network. Communication across a PVC did not require the call setup and termination states that were used with SVCs. 

Quality of service was also engineered into this solution. Frame Relay offered a Committed Information Rate (CIR) at which data could definitely be transmitted. If traffic and the SLA allow, then data could burst above the CIR to an Extended Information Rate (EIR). These frames were marked discard eligible (DE). Frames in excess of EIR were immediately dropped.
