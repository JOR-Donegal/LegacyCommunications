In previous notes on information theory, we saw that there are particular components in the communications medium. A simplified version of this would show a transmitter, a channel, and a receiver.

From 1793, much of Europe was at war with France, and Ireland was occupied by England. A symptom of the wars between the English and French were recurring attempts by the French to use Ireland as a “back door” into England. It was clear from the French activity from 1796 to 1798 that the threat of invasion in Ireland by the French and their allies was real. The ease with which the French evaded the British fleet, the unprepared defense forces and fortifications and the possibility of indigenous support and revolution all lead to fears of invasion by the authorities. 

Between 1804 and 1806 signal stations were established in a continuous line from Dublin, around the south coast and north to Malin Head. These facilitated (relatively!) rapid communications of orders and events to and from otherwise exposed and remote areas and followed similar developments in England and France. 

Captain Sir William Augustus Smith was assistant engineer in the Lough Swilly district from 1798 until his death in 1831. The original sketches for the defensive works at Lough Swilly from Smith dating from 1799 are held at Trinity College, Dublin in two volumes. It can be hard to get access, and it is a “gloves only” operation. 

At Lough Swilly, towers were constructed at Fanad Head and at Malin Head and their site, original construction and development are dated and well portrayed by the sketches of Sir William Smith.

Why the history lesson? 

The title of this document is Legacy Communications. I can establish through primary sources that we had digital, semaphore-based communications operating at the speed of light from Donegal to Dublin from c. 1804, a Wide Area Network (WAN) using free-space optics!
